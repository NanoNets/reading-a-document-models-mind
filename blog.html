We put our document AI in an MRI machine

Every day, people hand document models millions of pages and get clean data back in about a second. This is the story of what happens inside that second — watched live, for the first time on a document model, with an instrument that reads the model’s mind mid‑thought.

MS Mohammad Ali Shehral · Nanonets Applied AI Research· August 2026· Internal draft

The ritual, reconstructed with a demo invoice we rendered. Millions of exchanges like this run every day — finance teams, logistics desks, hospital back offices.

An invoice goes in. Clean data comes out. Somebody’s month‑end close moves one document forward, and nobody thinks about it again — which is exactly what good infrastructure feels like.

Now the question almost nobody asks: what happened during that one second in the middle?

Until recently, nobody could tell you

Not the people who use these models. Not, honestly, the people who build them — and we build them. If you grew up in the software era, that sounds impossible. It isn’t — it’s the defining property of the technology, and the strange deal the whole AI industry signed.

When ordinary software misbehaves, an engineer opens the code and finds the line that did it. An AI model breaks that deal completely: it is not a program anyone wrote. It’s billions of numbers that arranged themselves during training, and there is no code to open, no line to point to. The model plainly knows things — watch it flawlessly parse a table it has never seen — but it knows them in a language no human reads.

So the industry learned to treat models the way you treat a vending machine: judge them by what comes out. Feed in thousands of test documents, keep rigorous score, publish the accuracy. That outside‑in testing is real science — it’s how we publish our benchmarks — but it has a ceiling. It tells you what the model got right. It can never tell you why.

Then somebody built a scanner

This July, researchers at Anthropic — the AI lab behind Claude, ChatGPT’s main rival — published a way to look inside (their post is worth a visit for the video tour alone): an instrument that translates a model’s internal states into ordinary words while it thinks. Not a log of what the model typed — a readout of what it was thinking about, mid‑thought, including thoughts that never reach the page.

An MRI machine for a model’s brain, more or less.

To be precise about the metaphor: imaging, not telepathy. It shows structure in a computation the way an MRI shows structure in tissue — nobody here thinks the model has feelings. What it shows is what the computation is about, while it runs.

It came with one catch: it only worked on models that read text. Our models look at things — pixels, scans, photographed receipts — and nobody had fully validated such a scanner for a model with eyes. So we built one, validated it end to end, and pointed it at Nanonets‑OCR2, our open‑weights document model — meaning the billions of numbers themselves are published. We chose it deliberately: anyone can download the exact model we scanned, run our released code, and check every reading in this post.

Then we put the model in the scanner.

How do you scan a brain made of numbers?

To follow what comes next you need exactly one piece of anatomy, and it fits in a picture. A document model has eyes: a front end that chops the page into little tiles and turns each tile into numbers — a few hundred signals that now stand in for your invoice. Those signals then climb a stack of 35 thinking steps (engineers call them layers, which is why the scanner below labels them L 0 to L 34), each one refining the same evolving thought: what am I looking at, and what should I say about it? Only at the very top of the stack does thought become language — the final state is converted into a single word. Then the entire 35‑step climb runs again. And again — once per word, dozens of full climbs inside that one second you never think about.

Everything you have ever seen a model produce comes from the top of that stack. The other thirty‑four steps — where the actual understanding happens — are normally invisible. The scanner is a passive readout, not surgery: the model computes exactly what it always did. The difference is that now we get to watch.

Real measurements: each genre’s signature inner vocabulary; L is the median layer where it first appears.

Hand it an equation page and its inner monologue fills with LaTeX scaffolding — the bracket‑and‑backslash symbols of mathematicians’ typesetting language. Hand it a receipt and up comes TOTAL, plus separator art for the totals block. Handwriting summons half‑words — the model sounding out the script — and skips typesetting entirely, because prose needs none. The model doesn’t run one generic pipeline and note differences along the way. It becomes a different specialist for each kind of document, before writing anything.

And its inner voice is multilingual even when the document isn’t. On our expense‑form test, one late layer holds Employee, 员工, and empleado — the same idea in three languages, simultaneously. Somewhere in training it learned “employee‑ness” as something bigger than any one language.

It starts answering before it finishes reading

Two more readings off the scanner, because they change how you should picture that one‑second pause.

First: through the middle of its thinking, the model isn’t working in words at all yet. It’s working in schema — what this document is, which fields matter, what shape the answer takes — and its inner state matches the exact word it will eventually type only about one time in twenty at the low point. (The answer still comes out right; it just isn’t assembled into English until the end.) The exact wording snaps into place only in the last few steps — where, before a single character exists, the model is already spelling the first word of its answer. The pause isn’t hesitation. It’s rehearsal.

Second: hand it any page — invoice, equation, even handwriting — and in 26 out of 26 test documents the signal <table is already among its strongest internal readings while it’s still reading. A table reflex, whether or not there’s a table. Nobody coded that; it’s a professional habit the model trained into itself — the way an accountant’s eyes jump to the bottom line. Its base model shows that reflex twice in the whole study. Ours shows it 198 times.

88%of the most influential internal signals in the open base model have no name in its vocabulary — 73% in our own model, and in ours the effect sits even further above the calibration floor. The scanner’s most humbling reading: these models know far more than they say — words are just the export format. That nameless majority is where our reliability research lives.

Both twins in the scanner

Nanonets‑OCR2 is built from an open base model plus our document training — which let us do something genuinely new: put both in the scanner and compare minds, layer by layer. The picture is remarkably clean. Our training rewired the model’s early reading machinery — where document expertise lives — while leaving its writing machinery essentially untouched.

That’s what a specialized model is, visible at layer resolution for the first time on a document model: not a generic model with a prompt taped on, but one whose reading apparatus was rebuilt around documents. The scanner even catches the habits our training instilled — the document‑trained model keeps its attention on the page image about 3× harder than its untrained twin while answering — exactly the habit you want in a system whose job is to report what’s on the page, not what’s plausible. We taught it to keep its eyes on the document. It listened.

Why this matters to you

Because reliability starts becoming an engineering discipline — rather than a guessing game — when you can watch the machine think. An instrument like this is how document AI graduates from a black box you score to a system you can inspect — and inspection is the foundation everything else gets built on: better models, sharper evidence, automation you can defend to an auditor.

The full study — the method, every measurement, the fine print, and the complete interactive scan — is in our research note. The scanners themselves are open — researchers call them lenses: both fitted lenses, all the code, every evidence table. Fit them to your own documents and tell us what you find.
