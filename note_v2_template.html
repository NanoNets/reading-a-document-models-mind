You can’t read an AI model the way you read software

When a program misbehaves, someone opens the code and finds the line that did it. AI models broke that deal: a model that turns any scanned page into clean data is not a program anyone wrote, but billions of numbers that arranged themselves during training. Nothing inside is written in any human language. That is the famous black box, and AI interpretability is the field building microscopes for it.

In July, Anthropic published one of the best such microscopes to date: the Jacobian lens, which translates a model’s internal states into ordinary vocabulary and revealed what they call a global workspace — an inner desk where the model holds what it’s working on. (Their write‑up is worth ten minutes — it includes a video tour of the idea.) Their instrument existed only for text models. Ours look at pixels.

So we built the missing instrument — and pointed it at our own models.

Taking the lens multimodal is genuinely hard — backpropagating through a vision stack is expensive, library support is missing, and silent pitfalls make a naive fit produce garbage. As far as we are aware, ours is the first fully validated, full‑protocol lens fitted on real documents over a vision‑language model, the first such lens for a document model, and the first matched pair (base model + its Nanonets fine‑tune) — the pair is what makes the before‑and‑after study below possible.

Building the instrument — and proving it works

FIRST, THE PATIENT — WHAT A DOCUMENT MODEL ACTUALLY IS

If you have never looked inside one of these systems, here is the whole anatomy. A document model has eyes: a front end that cuts the page image into a grid of patches and turns each patch into a bundle of numbers — a few hundred signals standing in for the page. Those signals then pass through a stack of 35 thinking steps (the “layers”), each of which reads the current state of thought and refines it. Only after the top of the stack does anything become language: the final state is converted into one word, the word is appended, and the whole stack runs again for the next word. Everything you ever see from a model is that last conversion. The other 34 steps — where the actual understanding happens — are normally invisible.

The lens is exactly that coral tap, made rigorous. For each of the 35 steps it learns one translation — how thought at that depth tends to become language — and then holds the model still on a new document and reads. Three moves, end to end:

1

Fit

Learn, from 1,000 prompts — 900 real document pages plus 100 plain‑text passages — how each of the model’s 35 readable stages becomes words: one translator per stage, ~256 image patches + text per page.

2

Read

Freeze the model mid‑thought on a new document and translate any stage directly into vocabulary — no waiting for output.

3

Compare

Fit the identical instrument to the base model and to Nanonets‑OCR2, and diff the two minds layer by layer.

Before trusting one reading, we validated the hard way. First we reproduced Anthropic’s published result end‑to‑end on a small text model — our implementation clears the standard logit‑lens baseline on all six of their evaluation sets, with the same margin pattern — before ever fitting a vision model. Then, on the document lenses: two fits on disjoint halves of the corpus agree at 99.8–99.99% per layer, and every measurement carries in‑run calibration against noise floors and ceilings, cross‑checked against the released tables before reaching this page.

For the technically curious: the full protocol

Lens. For each layer ℓ, Jℓ is a corpus‑averaged linear map from that layer’s residual state to the final layer; a state h reads as softmax(WU·norm(Jℓh)). Corpus. 900 DocILE business‑document pages + 100 WikiText passages, four rotating instructions (“Transcribe this document as plain text.”, “Convert this document to markdown, preserving tables.”, “Extract all line items from this document as a table.”, “Read this document and reproduce its full text content.”). Fit. 8 interleaved 125‑prompt shards, fp32, certified merging; ~55 GPU‑hours for the base fit (~130 across the study); the OCR2 lens fitted on the interleaved 500‑prompt half. Models. Qwen2.5‑VL‑3B‑Instruct and Nanonets‑OCR2‑3B, pinned revisions. Validation. E0 baseline sweep 6/6 (typo margin 4.0×, multilingual 2.1×); shard‑stability CKA 0.998–0.9999; profile drift <0.001 with added data. Layer counts. The decoder has 36 blocks; the lens reads the 35 intermediate states between them, numbered L0–L34. Attention measurements index blocks directly, so an attention layer index can differ from a lens index by one. Full details, seeds, and job records ship in the release. Paper: transformer-circuits.pub/2026/workspace.

A tour of the mind

Under the lens, the 35 stages organize into three rooms: seeing (the pixels’ raw impression), understanding (the document as an idea), and writing (turning the plan into keystrokes). The understanding room is where it gets remarkable: each document genre summons its own inner vocabulary, before a single character of output.

__TAPESTRY__

Signature workspace vocabulary by genre, Nanonets‑OCR2 — each token appears for the genre’s documents and (almost) never for others. L = the median layer where it first surfaces in that genre.

Three residents of the workspace deserve special mention. First, the polyglot inner voice: 22 of our 26 test documents are English‑dominant, and every one of those 22 shows non‑Latin workspace tokens — on our expense‑form capture, layer 33 holds Employee, 员工, and empleado simultaneously: the same concept in three languages at once. Second, universal document instincts: Page and Date appear in the workspace of 26/26 documents — whatever the genre, a document mind orients by page and date. Third, the table reflex: <table reaches OCR2’s strongest signals on 26/26 documents (198 layer‑document cells; its base model: 2) — a specialist’s reflex, trained in.

The two-phase reader

Aggregating the whole test set reveals OCR2’s signature rhythm: a long understanding phase (layers ~9–21, 71–89% content words) followed by a typesetting phase (layers ~22–31) where the workspace turns to your output’s structure. And the typesetting phase is judicious: strongest for equations (~90% of top signals) and receipts (~79%), essentially absent for handwriting (1%) — which comes out as prose and needs none.

Two more measurements complete the picture. The model’s certainty ramps: its inner guess matches its final word only ~5–7% of the time through the understanding phase — it is thinking in schema, not words — then leaps to 55% just below the top — and locks in at the top itself, where the state becomes the output. And its eyes stay on the document: while answering, OCR2 puts 3.1× more attention on the page image than its base model, peaking at half of all attention around layer 21.

What the workspace knows — beyond what it says

The lens reads the parts of thinking that align with vocabulary. How much of the workspace is like that? We sorted thousands of the model’s most influential internal signals — the ones its own circuitry amplifies most — and asked which have names. In the heart of the workspace, most have no name at all — 88% in the open base model, 73% in Nanonets‑OCR2, the fine‑tune sitting even further above its calibration floor (calibrated against noise floors and ceilings; replicated in both models). Names arrive only near the writing room. Words are the export format; the thinking itself is richer.

And the rich part is readable by other means. Small trained pattern‑detectors (“probes”) decode the document’s type straight from the workspace with near‑perfect discrimination (AUC 0.96 over 400 documents), its currency at 0.86, and even how much of the page its table covers (R² 0.49). A separate 26‑document look at the vision tokens finds that their dominant axis mid‑network is simply how far down the page content sits (R² 0.66 at L19). A document model tracks far more about your document than its text output can express — and instruments like these read that hidden layer directly. That is where next‑generation reliability tooling comes from.

Fine-tuning, X-rayed

Because OCR2 shares its architecture and vocabulary with its open base model, the comparison is unusually clean: nothing in the readout is an artifact of a changed vocabulary. Layer by layer, our training rebuilt the reader and kept the typewriter: the translation machinery changes most in the early reading stages — where document expertise lives — and converges to 99.96% agreement at the output stages, while the models’ actual thoughts do the reverse.

How we did this — and where the edges are

Everything above comes from one instrument, one probe set, and a lot of paranoia. The instrument you know by now. The test set is 26 document images drawn from public datasets, spanning six genres. The pages shown live on this page are all cleared for reuse: three from the UCSF Industry Documents archive (via DocVQA), IRS Form W‑9, and pages we rendered ourselves — the demo invoice in the blog and the expense form behind the polyglot example. Genre findings are 4–6 documents per genre: directional and replicable rather than census‑grade.

The paranoia is the part we’re proudest of. Every reading is taken before the model produces any output; readouts are stable across independently fitted lenses (workspace‑band Jaccard ~0.91 between the merged lens and a single‑shard lens; all‑layer median 0.90); the nameless‑majority study runs on 1,500 directions per population per layer with criteria written down before the run; the probe studies use 400 documents with document‑level splits, independently audited for leakage; and the release includes the verified numbers table behind this page — every figure and claim with the evidence file it comes from — plus the raw tables themselves, each stamped with the code version that produced it. We think that trail is worth as much as the findings.

Where the edges are. The lens is a readout view of the state, not the state itself, and token identities in readouts depend on the fitting diet — so we lean on cross‑class contrasts, which hold up across independent fits. One model family is studied; genre samples are small; and the deepest finding cuts both ways: if 88% of the strongest signals have no name, then any vocabulary‑based reading — ours included — is the beginning of the story, not the end. That open edge is exactly where our research is headed next.

What does reading a mind actually buy you?

A fair question — the readings above are striking, but striking isn’t a business case. Three concrete things change when you can see inside.

Understanding becomes engineering. Outside‑in testing tells you what a model got right; an instrument like this starts telling you why. Once behavior has visible mechanics — schema assembling at one depth, formatting at another, attention holding on the page — those mechanics become things you can measure, monitor, and design for, the way every other engineering discipline works on its subject.

Training becomes verifiable. The base‑versus‑fine‑tune comparison is a before‑and‑after X‑ray of our own training: we can point at the layers where document expertise was installed, see the reading machinery rebuilt while the writing machinery is preserved, and confirm the model attends to the page harder because we trained it to. That’s a new kind of quality evidence — not a score on a benchmark, but a mechanism you can inspect.

The hidden 88% becomes a frontier. The lens’s most important reading is that most of what the model uses has no name in its vocabulary. Everything a document specialist knows beyond words — layout, structure, genre — lives in that nameless majority, and instruments that can read it are the obvious next thing to build. That is where our reliability research is headed.

FAQ

Why study OCR2 rather than your newest model?

Interpretability results are only worth publishing if others can reproduce them, which requires open weights. Nanonets‑OCR2 is our newest open‑weights model — anyone can download it and check every reading here. The instruments themselves are model‑agnostic; OCR2 is where the open, checkable science can live.

Is the interactive demo real?

Yes — every chip is a real measurement from Nanonets‑OCR2 reading that exact public document, captured before any output. Display edits: control tokens hidden, pure‑whitespace tokens condensed to one symbol.

Did Anthropic do this first?

Anthropic invented the lens and validated it on text models — this work builds directly on theirs, with credit and thanks. What didn’t exist before: a fully validated, full‑protocol lens fitted on real documents over a model that sees, the first such lens for a document model, and a matched base/fine‑tune pair. Those are ours.

Can I run this on my own documents?

Yes. The lenses, fitting code, and evaluation harness are Apache‑2.0. Reading a document through a fitted lens is a single forward pass on one GPU; re‑fitting from scratch takes about 55 GPU‑hours.

Does reading the model’s mind change what it does?

No. The lens is a passive readout: we freeze the model’s internal state on a document and translate a copy of it. Nothing is injected back, so the model computes exactly what it would have computed with no lens attached.

How is this different from just testing the model on thousands of documents?

They answer different questions, and we do both. Benchmarks measure what comes out; the lens shows how the answer took shape on the way there — which fields were assembled at which depth, when formatting was prepared, what the model held that it never typed. Benchmarks score the machine; instruments explain it.

Does this mean the model is conscious, or has feelings?

Nothing here supports that. What we measured is information processing with visible structure — recognition, planning, formatting — the way an fMRI shows structure in blood flow. “Mind,” “thinking,” and “workspace” are working vocabulary for that structure, not claims about experience.

Why do the readings look like single words rather than sentences?

Because the lens translates internal states through the model’s own vocabulary — the same list of word‑pieces it types with — so each reading is the set of vocabulary items a state most strongly points toward. That’s also the instrument’s known edge: thoughts with no single‑word name (88% of the strongest signals) show up as influence without a label, which is exactly the frontier we describe above.
