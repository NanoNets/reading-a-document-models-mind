You can’t read an AI model the way you read software

If you’ve spent your career around software, you know the deal: when a program does something surprising, someone opens the code and finds the line that did it. Software is written; whatever it does is written down somewhere.

AI models are different, and it surprises almost everyone meeting them up close for the first time. A model like the ones behind ChatGPT or Claude — or the Nanonets models that read documents — is not a program a person wrote. It is billions of numbers that arranged themselves during training. Open the file and there are no rules, no logic, no comments. The model clearly knows things — it reads invoices in forty languages — but nothing inside is written in any language humans read. That’s the famous black box, and it’s why a young scientific field called AI interpretability builds instruments that make those numbers legible — the way microscopes once made cells legible.

This July, Anthropic’s interpretability team published one of the most exciting instruments yet: the Jacobian lens. Pointed at any of the processing stages inside a language model, it translates the model’s internal state into ordinary vocabulary — a transcript of what the model is considering before it says anything. With it, they argued that language models keep a global workspace: an inner desk where the model holds what it’s working on.

Their instrument only existed for models that read text. Our models look at pixels. So we built the missing instrument — and pointed it at our own models.

Within days of Anthropic’s release, several groups tried to take the lens multimodal; every public attempt stalled at the hard parts — the GPU cost, the missing library support, the silent ways a naive fit produces garbage. Ours is, as far as an exhaustive search can establish, the first fully‑validated, full‑protocol lens fitted on real documents over a vision‑language model — the first for a document model of any kind, and the first matched pair (a base model and its Nanonets fine‑tune), which is what makes the before‑and‑after story below possible.

The instrument, and how we know it works

Think of the lens as a translator that learned, from a thousand example documents, how each middle stage of the model eventually becomes words — so it can translate the middle stages into words directly, without waiting for the model to finish. Building it for a model with eyes meant rewiring the method around a second sense: our models take in roughly 250 patches of pixels per page alongside the prompt.

We validated it the hard way before trusting a single reading: on the six evaluation sets Anthropic published with the method, our implementation beats the standard baseline on all six with the same margin pattern they report; fitted twice on completely different documents, the two lenses agree to 99.9%; and every measurement carries built‑in calibration against noise floors and ceilings, checked adversarially before anything reached this page.

For the technically curious: what the lens actually is

For each layer ℓ, the lens is a corpus‑averaged linear map Jℓ from that layer’s residual state to the model’s final layer; a state h is read as softmax(WU·norm(Jℓh)). We fitted 8 disjoint 125‑document shards with certified merging (~55 GPU‑hours for the base fit, ~130 across the study), fp32 with checkpoint/resume. Convergence: disjoint‑corpus fits agree at cosine 0.997–0.999; the layer‑profile drifts by at most 0.001 adding shards. The fitting corpus, code, and evaluation harness are all in the open release below.

A tour of the mind

Under the lens, the model’s 35 processing stages organize into three rooms — you just scrubbed through them above.

Seeing (early layers). The first stages hold the pixels’ raw impression. Readings here are murky — the model is looking, not yet thinking.

Understanding (middle layers, ~10–30). Here the document assembles as an idea. Show the model a receipt and — before it has typed a single character — its workspace fills with Date, Name, Email, Address, Page: the fields it expects to need, most of them not written anywhere on the page. Show it equations and the workspace fills with mathematical scaffolding; handwriting evokes prose. The model has recognized what it is holding and laid out its plan. We call this the silent schema — the plan you can read a dozen steps before the first keystroke.

Writing (final layers, ~31+). The last stages turn the plan into keystrokes — and in our OCR model you can watch something remarkable: the workspace fills with table markup, rules, and separators. The model is laying out your output format — the exact HTML table structure it will deliver — before writing a word of it. That is a document specialist at work.

Models know more than they say — and now we can see it

Here is the discovery that turned our replication into new research. The lens translates internal states into vocabulary — so it can only show the parts of thinking that align with words. We measured how much of the workspace is like that, across thousands of the model’s most influential internal signals.

In the heart of the workspace, up to 88% of the model’s most influential signals have no name in its vocabulary. Words are the export format — the thinking itself is richer.

That number is why this research program matters commercially: a document model tracks far more about your document — layout, geometry, structure, confidence — than its text output can express. Instruments like ours read that hidden layer directly. It is the difference between judging a model by what it says and seeing what it knows — and it is exactly where next‑generation reliability tooling comes from: catching disagreements between what a model represents and what it reports, before they reach your ERP.

For the technically curious: the measurement

We sort directions by a weights‑only broadcast score (how strongly the model’s own circuitry amplifies them), then measure nameability as the maximum cosine to any lens vector, with an in‑run calibration ceiling (lens vectors score 1.0) and a covariance‑matched noise floor (≈0.08). At the strongest workspace layer, 88% of top‑broadcast directions sit at the floor (+22 points over base rate, p < 10−4); the pattern replicates in both the base model and our fine‑tune, and only reverses near the output layers.

What Nanonets training builds into a model

Because our OCR model is a fine‑tune of an open base model, we could do something no one has done before: fit the lens to both, and diff the minds. Same architecture, same vocabulary — the only difference is our document training. Watching what that training built is the closest thing yet to an X‑ray of fine‑tuning:

It rebuilt the reader and kept the typewriter. Layer by layer, the two models’ translation machinery differs most in the early reading stages and converges to 99.96% agreement at the output stages — while their actual thoughts do the reverse. Our training rewired how the model takes documents in, precisely where document expertise lives, and left the writing machinery stable.

It moved document structure to the front of the mind. Field schema surfaces earlier at every step, and the late workspace — the stage right before writing — is given over to the output’s structure: up to 100% of the strongest readings there are table formatting. The generalist thinks about many things; the specialist has already moved on to delivering yours in the format you asked for.

It tuned the model for documents through and through. The clearest single image from the whole study: the base chat model spends half its network attending to conversational bookkeeping — our training redirects that capacity to document structure, leaving its grip on table markup untouched, indeed the strongest‑aligned channel we measured in either model.

Why this matters if you buy document AI

Models that handle your documents have rich inner lives their outputs don’t show: plans they’ve made, structure they track, expertise their training carved into them. A vendor that can see those inner lives can verify what a model actually learned, measure what a fine‑tune actually changed, and build reliability tooling on what the model knows rather than only on what it says. That is the standard we hold our own models to — and this note is what holding that standard looks like in practice.

FAQ

Why study OCR2 rather than your newest model?

Interpretability results are only worth publishing if others can reproduce them, and that requires open weights. Nanonets‑OCR2 is our newest open‑weights model — anyone can download it and check every reading on this page. The same instruments run internally on our production models.

Is the interactive demo above real?

Yes — every chip is a real measurement from Nanonets‑OCR2 reading that exact document, captured before the model produced any output. The only display edit: pure‑whitespace tokens are condensed to a single symbol.

Did Anthropic do this first?

Anthropic invented the lens and validated it on text models — this work builds directly on theirs, with credit and thanks. What didn’t exist before: a validated lens for a model that sees, a lens for a document model, and a matched base/fine‑tune pair. Those are ours.

Can I run this on my own documents?

Yes. The lenses, fitting code, and evaluation harness are released under Apache‑2.0. A consumer GPU can read documents through a fitted lens in seconds; re‑fitting from scratch takes about 55 GPU‑hours.
